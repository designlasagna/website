How to make a guide page feel useful at a glance.

This layout is meant for practical articles: setup instructions, “how we work” pages, and guides that need to stay readable even when the reader is scanning for one answer.

How-toStep-by-stepReference-friendly

1 Start with a promise.

The headline should tell readers what they will be able to do by the end. For example: “Set up tokens in your editor,” or “Write a docs page that is easy to scan.”

2 Set up the page with wayfinding.

Use a TOC, short section labels, and visible hierarchy. The reader should always know where they are and what comes next.

  • Keep the main column narrow enough to read.
  • Use anchors for every meaningful section.
  • Keep navigation adjacent, not dominant.

3 Let type do the heavy lifting.

Big title, small eyebrow, generous line height. This page uses a magazine-grade heading system, but the body copy stays quiet and approachable.

Tip

If your guide has more than one idea, break it into numbered chunks instead of stacking paragraphs.

4 Add supporting components.

Notes, code blocks, quick facts, and side cards are excellent here. They help the reader skim without losing the shape of the article.

5 End with a next action.

A guide is more useful when it points somewhere specific: the docs, the writing archive, or the tool that solves the problem.

Quick facts

Use when: the page is instructional and readers want to jump around.

Works well for: onboarding, tutorials, setup docs, and process pages.

Pattern: intro → steps → tips → related links.
